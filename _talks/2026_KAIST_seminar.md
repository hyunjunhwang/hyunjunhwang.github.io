The planning and policy decisions that behavioral models are built to support are counterfactual by nature, and must be justified to stakeholders before they are made. There is therefore a class of problems that predictive precision alone does not settle. This talk revisits interpretability as the property that class of problems requires, and pursues it through walking, a mode that resists assumptions motorized travel tolerates.

Two lines of econometric modeling work are introduced. The first is more empirical, extending established model structures so that the quantities of interest become separately identifiable: how the determinants of walking shifted across the pandemic, and how its multiple dimensions relate once shared unobserved influences are accounted for. The second is more methodological, developing a spatial model in which the reach of each built-environment influence is estimated rather than assumed, with direct implications for the scale at which pedestrian policy should act. The talk closes with directions in which interpretable specifications can absorb new forms of data and relax remaining structural assumptions.
